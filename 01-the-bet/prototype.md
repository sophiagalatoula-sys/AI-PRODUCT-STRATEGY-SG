# The Prototype Bet

## What I Built
<!-- One sentence: what does this prototype demonstrate? -->
An AI-enhanced credit risk model for Greece BNPL, prototyped as a three-screen Credit Decision Console. At its core, the model's job is feature selection: figuring out which signals actually predict repayment among everything Helios Pay has available, including signals the bureau has no visibility into at all, since they're collected directly by Helios Pay and never shared outward — so the credit score can both approve genuinely creditworthy customers the bureau's more conservative, one-size-fits-all view would have rejected, and decline risky customers the bureau's score would have wrongly approved and who would otherwise go on to default. Two kinds of signal feed it: customer-profile data (age, gender, education, marital status, income, existing debt and expenses, and digital-footprint signals — device and browser fingerprinting, and indicators like how many platforms or accounts an applicant is associated with, none of which the bureau ever sees) and transactional data (prior BNPL and general-purpose loan history with Helios Pay, on-time repayment record, and purchase behavior — merchant, category, and amount). Today's scorecard already blends some of these Helios-Pay-only digital signals in alongside the bureau's own characteristics, but weights them far below the bureau's view — the AI model's contribution is to relearn that weighting from what the data actually predicts, rather than treat the bureau's characteristics as the default and everything else as an afterthought.

The model also changes when a Tiresias bureau call happens at all, not just what happens with the result. Two circumstances skip a fresh, billable call entirely: a repeat applicant whose repayment behavior with Helios Pay is already proven, where Helios Pay's own evidence can carry the decision on its own, and an applicant Tiresias was already queried for within the last 60 days, where the existing bureau data is reused rather than paid for again. Neither case requires resolving the bigger, separate question of whether Tiresias is needed for BNPL at all — Credit Risk currently favors keeping it, though the business sees Klarna's own bureau-free Greek BNPL operation as a real, unresolved case worth assessing — it just means being deliberate today about when a new call is actually necessary.

Screen one shows how that model actually gets used day to day, not just what it scores. Sending every application to manual review isn't workable — the underwriting team isn't large enough, and a BNPL decision has to come back instantly — so a second, holistic-judgment model reads the applicant's full record (raw profile, transaction history, digital-footprint signals, repayment history) alongside the new model's own score and decision, and independently judges whether it agrees. When it does — the large majority of applications — the decision is applied automatically; the legacy bureau verdict is still shown alongside as reference context, but disagreeing with it is no longer what triggers review. Only when the holistic-judgment model's own read disagrees with the new model's decision does the application route to an underwriter, together with the model's justification for why it disagrees. That's also how the model gets refined on exactly the cases it's least sure about: the underwriter's confirm-or-correct call on each routed case is training signal. The screen lets an analyst toggle between three examples — auto-decided, routed, and a third case where an already auto-decided applicant contacts customer support to ask about their decision — showing the legacy score and verdict, the new model's score and verdict, and the holistic-judgment model's justification in every case, rather than asking them to trust a black-box override. That third example is the same screen doing double duty: it's not only the routing queue for the roughly 5% flagged in real time, it's also where an underwriter re-opens an already-decided case on request, re-examines the same record and justification, and confirms or corrects it. Screen two shows what this is meant to move at the portfolio level: the approval-rate gap, the higher-risk segment's default rate, and the auto-decided/routed split itself — how many applications clear automatically, and, for the routed minority, what approval rate and default rate that segment sees after underwriter review. Screen two also carries two pre-launch validation reports for the new scorecard specifically — a score-band distribution table showing the auto-approved/auto-declined/routed split within each band, and a decline-reasons breakdown by share of all declines — confirming the new scorecard behaves as designed across its full range before it replaces the legacy one, and continuing to run afterward as an ongoing check against drift. Screen two also surfaces a monthly review panel: combining two signals — how often underwriters confirm the holistic-judgment model's flags, and actual repayment outcomes once loans mature — it flags candidates for the underwriting policy's next review — a characteristic worth adding, one worth dropping, or a weight worth reconsidering. It doesn't change the live scorecard itself; any adopted change still goes through Credit Risk's existing review process. Refreshed monthly rather than per application, so every applicant in between is still decided against the same, consistent underwriting policy. Screen three shows the applicant's own outcome, approved or declined, in plain language.

## The Bet in One Sentence
<!-- What you're building, for whom, why now -->
For a BNPL lender sitting on years of its own customer-profile and transaction data that the external credit bureau never sees, we're betting that using AI to identify which of those signals actually predict repayment — auto-deciding the large majority of applications, and routing to a small underwriting team only the minority where a second, holistic-judgment model's independent read of the full applicant record disagrees with the new model's own decision — both approves more of the customers the legacy scorecard wrongly rejects and declines more of the customers it wrongly approves who go on to default, preventing losses in the higher-risk segment, cutting the cost of redundant bureau calls for applicants Helios Pay already knows, without demanding a review volume no underwriting team could sustain or slowing down BNPL's need for an instant decision.

## Kill Criteria
<!-- When would you stop? What evidence would kill this bet? -->
Kill this bet if, after a 90-day pilot on the higher-risk segment, the new model's decisions don't cut that segment's default rate by at least a third relative to today's rate, or if underwriters overturn the new model's own decision in more than half of the cases the holistic-judgment model routes for review — evidence the model's signal selection isn't sound enough to trust, which would make auto-deciding the rest of the volume unsafe. Also kill it if the pilot's overall portfolio approval rate drops by more than a couple of points versus today's baseline, since recreating the original over-conservatism this initiative exists to fix would undo the entire strategic point of building it.
