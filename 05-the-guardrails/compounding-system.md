# Compounding System Design

## Feedback Loops

| Loop | Input | Output | Compounds? | Status |
|------|-------|--------|-----------|--------|
| Recursive Learning | Underwriter decisions and justifications on the ~5% of applications routed for review, sampled in the existing weekly gold-set audit | Confirmed decisions become new golden-dataset rows; overturned ones flag a miss and feed the monthly Portfolio review model's characteristic-reweighting recommendations to Credit Risk | N | broken — the data-collection half runs on schedule, but nothing requires Credit Risk to act on a given month's recommendations, so the loop generates signal without a guaranteed path into an actual scorecard change |
| Cross-Domain Transfer | The holistic-judgment model's read of purchase amount, category, and merchant for every individual application in the pilot, which covers all applications rather than a single segment — whether this specific application's merchant, category, and amount should count as scoring signal, not what that merchant's whole history looks like across every application it's had (see Network Intelligence (merchant risk), below, for that). Category and merchant already carry narrow, fraud/dispute-only rules today (specific flagged merchants, the mobile category); amount carries none. All three are being assessed for broader, direct inclusion as credit-risk inputs in the new scorecard | A signal that's included is tested through the same pre-launch validation gate as the rest of the scorecard; a signal not yet included is meant to be surfaced by the holistic-judgment model as a recommendation to add it | N | broken — the same root cause as Recursive Learning above: nothing defines a cadence or a channel that commits Credit Risk to act on that recommendation once the holistic-judgment model makes it |
| Network Intelligence (fraud) | Device and browser fingerprints across all recent applications, cross-referenced against each other as the applicant pool grows | A shared-identity match routes an application to underwriter review regardless of what the applicant's own score would otherwise suggest; detection gets sharper as there are more applications to cross-reference against | Y | active |
| Network Intelligence (segment calibration) | Repayment and default outcomes across the full applicant population — not only routed cases — aggregated monthly by the Portfolio review model as the applicant base grows | Segment-level recalibration candidates for Credit Risk: evidence that a whole segment's scoring assumptions are off, not just that one flagged case was wrong | N | broken — same root cause as Recursive Learning and Cross-Domain Transfer above: the evidence compounds every month as more applicants generate outcomes, but nothing commits Credit Risk to act on what it shows |
| Network Intelligence (merchant risk) | None | None — no mechanism aggregates outcome patterns (fraud, disputes, early defaults) across Helios Pay's own merchant network as more merchants and transactions join it, the way the fraud-fingerprint check already does for applicants | N | missing |


**Broken loop identified by partner:** The same root cause repeats across three rows above — Recursive Learning, Cross-Domain Transfer, and Network Intelligence (segment calibration) — which makes it the table's dominant finding, not three unrelated quirks. Each one produces a real, scheduled recommendation (a reweighting candidate, a signal worth adding, a segment recalibration), and each one lets that recommendation sit indefinitely because nothing commits Credit Risk to record a decision on it. A correction identified in one month can go un-acted-on for as long as nobody happens to build the case for it, while every new applicant matching that same pattern keeps getting the old treatment.

**Fix plan:** Give every recommendation a deadline and a consequence for missing it, not just a request. Every candidate the monthly Portfolio review model flags — whether a reweighting suggestion, a new-signal recommendation, or a segment recalibration — gets a required adopt / defer / reject decision from Credit Risk within that same monthly cycle, logged alongside the golden dataset the same way underwriter decisions already are. If the cycle closes with no recorded decision, the candidate doesn't silently reset to zero — it carries forward as overdue at the top of next month's report, and a running overdue-candidate count becomes its own tracked line in the same audit cadence already in place, so an unclosed backlog is visible to whoever already reviews that audit rather than something someone has to go looking for. Name a specific accountable owner within Credit Risk for closing these out each cycle, the same way a rotating on-call underwriter is already named for routed-case review.

## Context Connectivity

Customer-support-escalated reviews feed the same training pipeline as real-time-routed decisions: an underwriter's confirm-or-correct call on an escalated case is captured through the same weekly gold-set audit that turns routed-case decisions into golden-dataset rows and Portfolio-review input. That path isn't a silo.

The clearer candidate sits between the BNPL product side and Credit Risk: merchant- and category-level risk signals the product team observes — which merchants or purchase categories see disproportionate defaults or disputes — don't have a defined channel into Credit Risk's scorecard-design process today, separate from whatever the holistic-judgment model itself picks up from transaction data alone.


## Governance Policy

**Scope:** All AI components in Helios Pay's consumer credit-decisioning pipeline — the primary scorecard, the generated decision explanation, the holistic-judgment review model, and the monthly Portfolio review model. Does not cover other AI use elsewhere in the business, such as internal tooling, marketing, or support.

**Autonomy boundaries:** On the auto-decide path (roughly 95% of applications), the scorecard's decision and its generated explanation are sent to the applicant directly, with no human checkpoint. On the routed path (roughly 5%, or any application submitted while a reliability alert is open), the decision is not finalized or sent until a rotating on-call underwriter has reviewed it and signed off.

**Escalation triggers:** The holistic-judgment model disagreeing with the scorecard's decision; a shared-device-fingerprint match across recent applications; a data-validation failure on a required field; an open accuracy or hallucination-rate alert, during which every new application routes to underwriter review rather than auto-deciding, not only the ones that triggered the alert.

**Audit cadence:** Weekly, owned by the credit risk team — the golden-dataset accuracy and hallucination-rate run, plus a sample of that week's underwriter-routed cases. Monthly, same owner — the Portfolio review model's comparison of decisioning outcomes against applicant characteristics, plus a required adopt / defer / reject decision on every candidate it flags, recorded within that same monthly cycle by a named accountable owner within Credit Risk. A candidate left without a recorded decision doesn't reset — it carries forward as overdue at the top of the next month's report, and a running overdue-candidate count is itself a tracked line in this same monthly review, so an unclosed backlog is visible to whoever already reviews it.

**Regulatory exposure (EU AI Act / other):** 
High-risk under the EU AI Act — Article 6(2) and Annex III, point 5(b) name the evaluation of natural persons' creditworthiness and the establishment of a credit score explicitly. Most of the obligations that classification triggers map onto work this pipeline already has in place, even though none of it was originally built with the Act's article numbers in mind; three rows below are still a genuine gap — nothing has actually been built or decided for any of them yet, though Art. 10 now has a concrete first step identified.

| Obligation | Article | What it requires | Existing control | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classification | Art. 6(2), Annex III(5)(b) | Determines whether the system counts as high-risk, which is what triggers every obligation below | Creditworthiness assessment of natural persons | Confirmed high-risk |
| Risk management system | Art. 9 | A continuous, documented process across the system's lifecycle to identify, estimate, and mitigate its risks, including testing before the system (or a significant change to it) goes live | The vendor stress-testing and swap-readiness work, the reliability alert thresholds, a one-time pre-launch validation gate against the golden dataset before the new scorecard replaces the legacy one, and a required adopt / defer / reject decision from Credit Risk on every candidate the monthly Portfolio review model flags — with a named accountable owner, an overdue-candidate count tracked inside the existing audit cadence, and unresolved candidates carried forward visibly rather than silently reset | Met — identifying risk (the weekly and monthly audits, the escalation triggers), estimating it (the golden dataset's accuracy, hallucination-rate, and drift-velocity measurements), and mitigating it (rollback, routing, and now a guaranteed decision on every flagged recommendation) are all documented and running on a continuous cadence, closing the gap the previous pass in this table left open |
| Data and data governance | Art. 10 | Training, validation, and testing data must be relevant, sufficiently representative, and examined for bias and errors | No confirmed process — Credit Risk's annual scorecard review with Statistical Decisions Hellas may touch representativeness or bias informally, but that isn't confirmed as an actual, documented practice | Gap — the training population is never tested for representativeness or bias, only the model's outputs are, via the golden dataset. The concrete first step is Credit Risk and Legal jointly reviewing whether age, gender, and marital status should remain direct scoring inputs at all, rather than this repo or the model deciding unilaterally: gender carries confirmed EU regulatory exposure — Directive 2004/113/EC prohibits sex-based discrimination in access to financial services, reinforced by the CJEU's *Test-Achats* ruling banning sex as an actuarial factor in insurance from December 2012 — while age and marital status rest on weaker or unconfirmed protection at EU level and need a Greek-law check rather than being assumed either way |
| Technical documentation | Art. 11 | A technical file describing the system's design, intended purpose, data, performance, and risk management, kept up to date | Covered narratively across this repo — design, purpose, data, performance, risk | Partially met — not yet organized into the Act's required structure |
| Record-keeping (logging) | Art. 12 | Automatic logging of the system's operation, detailed enough to trace and reconstruct any individual decision after the fact | Every application's inputs, score, and how the score was calculated are logged at decision time — not only routed cases — and any underwriter can retrieve and review that record afterward, including when an applicant contacts customer support | Met |
| Transparency to deployers | Art. 13 | Instructions for use that let the deployer understand the system's capabilities, limitations, and correct operation | — | Gap — depends on how the provider/deployer split with the platform vendor is drawn, not yet worked out |
| Human oversight | Art. 14 | The system must be designed so a human overseer understands it, watches for errors, and — for any individual case — can choose not to use, override, or reverse its output | The routed queue, the reliability alerts that pause auto-decide system-wide, the weekly/monthly audits, and the customer-support-triggered look-back | Substantially met — everything to the left is real, but on the ~95% auto-decided path no human is positioned to intervene on that specific application before it ships; oversight there is systemic (alerts, audits) and after the fact (routing, support), not a person checking each case before it goes out |
| Accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity | Art. 15 | The system must perform at an appropriate accuracy level, before and after deployment, and resist errors, faults, and attempts to manipulate it | The reliability targets' four metrics, the golden dataset's regression testing, and the pre-launch validation gate confirming the new scorecard scores correctly against its agreed design before go-live | Met |
| Deployer obligations | Art. 26 | The deployer must assign competent human oversight, monitor for malfunction, and inform affected people that they are subject to the system | — | Gap — applicants aren't yet proactively told they're subject to an automated decision |
| Right to explanation | Art. 86 | A person subject to a high-risk decision affecting them can obtain a clear, meaningful explanation of the AI's role and the decision's main factors | Every applicant already receives a plain-language explanation of their own decision automatically, naming the driving factor(s), without needing to ask; an applicant who wants more can escalate through customer support to an underwriter, who re-examines the same logged record and communicates back | Met |

Also subject to GDPR Article 22, which restricts a decision "based solely on automated processing" that has a legal effect on the person unless an exemption applies — the auto-decide path is exactly that kind of decision, and the Article 86 control above is what an affected applicant actually uses in practice. Also subject to the EU Consumer Credit Directive's requirement to state the genuine reason for a declined application, which is the same requirement the explanation model's rollback safeguard already exists to protect, rather than falling back to generic templated text.

**Roadmap Brief:** Five of the six workarounds trace back to the same root cause — a capability gap, not a trust or pricing problem. People aren't going around Helios Pay's product because they distrust what it produces; they've found things it doesn't do at all yet. The one exception is workflow, not capability: customer support already has the right answer, just not in a place that's faster to use than AI. The two workarounds worth ignoring for now aren't low-value, they're blocked by the same regulatory boundary this repo already tracks elsewhere — anything that edges toward giving applicants forward-looking, individualized guidance risks becoming a second regulated decision surface under Art. 6(2) and Annex III, a governance question to resolve deliberately rather than a feature to ship quietly. A second pattern sits in the Signal source column itself: four of the six were only ever surfaced through informal internal observation, not through any dedicated support-ticket tag, forum monitoring, or tool-usage review built to catch this. Shadow AI discovery itself is ad hoc at Helios Pay today — the workarounds aren't the only gap; so is the mechanism for finding them.
